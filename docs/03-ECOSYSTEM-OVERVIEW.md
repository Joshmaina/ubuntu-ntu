# How UBUNTU-NTU Works — A Plain-Language Guide

> **Who this is for:** Everyone. No technical background needed. If you are a learner, a
> contributor, an elder considering recording your voice, a teacher, a partner, or a
> curious family member — this is the document to read.
>
> No jargon. Where a technical term is unavoidable, it is explained in the sentence
> where it first appears.

---

## In one paragraph

UBUNTU-NTU is a phone app for learning African languages. What makes it different is
that it teaches you to *hear and speak* rather than just read, it works without internet,
and the lessons are recorded by real people from the actual language community — not
generated by a computer. Everything those people record is also saved into a permanent
public archive, so the language is preserved even for people who never use the app.

---

## Part 1 — What it is like to learn with it

### You choose a language, and a variety of that language

Languages are not uniform. Yoruba as spoken in Oyo differs from Yoruba spoken in Ijebu.
Neither is more correct. You choose the one your family speaks, and you hear that one —
not a flattened "standard" version that sounds like nobody in particular.

### You download a lesson pack once, then use it anywhere

Before you start, the app downloads a small package of lessons — a few megabytes,
roughly the size of a handful of photographs. After that, **you do not need internet at
all.** Not to start a lesson, not to check an answer, not to hear the audio, not to
record your voice.

This matters more than it might sound. It means you can practise on a bus with no
signal, in a village with no coverage, or on a phone with no data left. Your progress is
saved on your own phone. Next time you happen to be connected, it quietly catches up
with the server in the background. You do not have to think about it.

### Lessons are short

Three to five minutes. Five questions or so. Small enough to do while waiting for
something else. The aim is a habit you keep, not a marathon you abandon.

### How a sentence is actually taught

This is the heart of the app, so it is worth walking through carefully.

Most language apps play you a sentence in the new language and ask you to work it out.
For African languages this fails badly, because the word order is often completely
different from English and the sentence goes past too fast to separate into words. You
freeze. You feel stupid. You close the app.

We do it in four steps instead. Say the sentence is *"I am going to the market"* in
Yoruba.

**Step 1 — You are told what it means, in your own language.**

> 🔊 *"I am going to the market."*

That is all. You now know the meaning. Your mind is no longer working on that problem,
so it is free to work on the next one.

**Step 2 — You see it broken into its smallest pieces.**

| Mo | ń | lọ | sí | ọjà |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | *(happening now)* | go | to | market |

Notice that "ń" does not translate to an English word at all — it is a piece that marks
the action as happening now. English does this with "am ...ing". Seeing this is how you
start to feel how the language is put together, rather than memorising phrases whole.

You can tap any piece to hear just that piece on its own.

**Step 3 — You hear it slowly.**

> 🔊 *"Mo... ń lọ... sí... ọjà."*

Half speed, with small gaps. Each piece lights up as it is spoken. This is where your ear
learns where one word ends and the next begins — the thing that is impossible at full
speed when everything sounds like one long stream.

**Step 4 — You hear it at natural speed.**

> 🔊 *"Mo ń lọ sí ọjà."*

Now it is spoken the way a person would actually say it. And because you have been
carried through the first three steps, it sounds like a sentence rather than noise.

**Then you build it yourself.** The pieces appear shuffled at the bottom of the screen
and you put them in the right order. You are not recalling a memorised string — you are
assembling a structure you have understood.

### Seeing your own tone

In many African languages — Yoruba, Igbo, Zulu, Twi, Kikuyu and many others — **the
pitch of your voice changes the meaning of the word.** The same syllables said at a
different pitch is a completely different word. This is the single hardest thing for
learners whose first language does not work that way, and almost no app teaches it.

Being told "your tone was wrong" is useless. You cannot hear what you did wrong, so you
cannot fix it.

So we show it to you instead. You record yourself, and the app draws two lines:

```text
   high  ┤     ╭──╮
         │    ╱    ╲              ━━━  the native speaker
         │   ╱      ╲             ╌╌╌  you
    mid  ┤ ╌╱╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╲╌╌
         │╱            ╲
    low  ┤              ╲___
         └────────────────────
```

Where the lines sit apart, your tone is off. Where they lie on top of each other, you
have it. You try again and watch them move closer. It becomes something you can see and
correct, like adjusting your aim.

**A fair comparison.** Voices differ — some people speak naturally low, others high. The
app does not compare your raw pitch to the speaker's. It compares the *shape* of your
pitch relative to your own normal voice. A deep voice and a high voice both get a fair
reading. You are never penalised for the voice you have.

### The app remembers what you are about to forget

Behind the scenes the app tracks every word you have learned and works out roughly when
you are likely to forget it. Then it shows you that word again just before that happens.

Recall a word easily, and it waits much longer before asking again. Struggle, and it
brings the word back soon. Over time you review only what you actually need to, which is
why a few minutes a day is enough to hold hundreds of words.

You never have to manage any of this. It just decides what to show you.

### Learning what to say, not just how to say it

In many African cultures, greeting an elder and greeting a friend are not the same act.
Using the wrong form is not a small grammatical error — it is disrespect, and people
notice.

So the lessons teach situations, not just vocabulary: how to greet an elder, how to buy
something in a market, how to answer when someone asks about your family. Proverbs are
taught with their meaning and the situations they are used in, because a proverb
translated literally is nonsense and translated properly is wisdom.

## Part 2 — Where the lessons come from

Here is the part most people do not expect.

**The lessons are not written by a computer, and not by us.** We build the system. The
language comes from the people who speak it.

### Contributing your voice

If you speak the language, you can record for it. You are shown a phrase, you tap and
say it, and you are done. It takes a few seconds. You can do ten in a couple of minutes
while waiting for a matatu.

### How we make sure it is right

A recording does not go straight into the app. Other speakers of the same language hear
it first and answer a simple question: *does this recording match this phrase, in this
variety of the language?*

Only after several people independently agree does it become a lesson. It takes one
person to make a mistake and several to agree — so the second is much more reliable.

If you record something that turns out wrong, nothing bad happens to you. It simply does
not get published, and often someone will record a correction.

### Your name stays with your voice

When a learner hears a phrase, they can see who recorded it:

> 🔊 *"Mo ń lọ sí ọjà."* — spoken by **Adunni**, Ibadan

This is deliberate. It is your voice, your language, and your contribution. You are not
anonymous raw material for someone else's product. If you would rather not be named, you
can contribute anonymously — but the default is that you are credited.

### Elders and oral knowledge

There is a particular place in this for older speakers, who often hold vocabulary,
proverbs, and stories that younger speakers have never heard. An elder can record a short
story or a piece of oral history. Learners can hear it, slow it down, and read along.

Some of what gets recorded this way may not exist anywhere else in the world.

## Part 3 — The archive

Everything that passes validation goes into a permanent public archive: the recording,
what it says, what it means, and how it breaks into pieces.

The archive is public and free, and it is separate from the app. That is deliberate, and
it is important:

**If this project ends, the archive remains.** If the app is never popular, if funding
never arrives, if the team stops — the recorded language survives, freely available to
anyone: researchers, teachers, the community itself, or someone building something better
in twenty years.

We are not asking people to trust that our product will succeed. The preservation happens
the moment a recording is validated, regardless of what happens to us.

## Part 4 — Who owns the language

A fair question, and one that deserves a direct answer.

**Not us.**

Language belongs to the community that speaks it. We look after a collection of
recordings. That is a responsibility, not ownership. In practice:

- You decide whether your recording is used, and you can withdraw it.
- We do not sell community recordings to companies.
- The community can dispute how their language is represented, and correct it.
- Consent is explained in the contributor's own language, because consent given in a
  language you do not fully read is not consent.
- Nothing is collected before these rules are published — not after.

There is a long and unfinished history of African communities being studied, recorded,
and extracted from by outsiders who took the results and gave nothing back. We are aware
of that history and we would rather be judged against it explicitly than quietly repeat
it.

The full rules are in [09-DATA-GOVERNANCE.md](09-DATA-GOVERNANCE.md).

## Part 5 — The whole cycle

```text
        ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │                                                 │
        ▼                                                 │
  ┌───────────┐      ┌────────────┐      ┌───────────┐   │
  │  Someone  │─────►│   Others    │─────►│  Becomes  │   │
  │  records  │      │   check it  │      │ a lesson  │   │
  └───────────┘      └────────────┘      └─────┬─────┘   │
                                                │         │
                            ┌───────────────────┘         │
                            ▼                             │
                     ┌────────────┐                       │
                     │  Learners  │───────────────────────┘
                     │   learn    │   corrections & requests
                     └─────┬──────┘   feed back in
                           │
                           ▼
                  ┌──────────────────┐
                  │  PUBLIC ARCHIVE  │  ← permanent, free, open
                  └──────────────────┘
```

Each person who records makes the app better for every learner. Each learner who spots
an error makes the archive more accurate. And everything validated is preserved
permanently, whatever happens to the app.

That is the whole idea: **learning a language and saving a language become the same
activity.**

## Common questions

**Do I need internet?**
Only to download lessons the first time, and occasionally to sync. Everything else works
completely offline.

**How much data does it use?**
A lesson pack is a few megabytes — comparable to sending a few photos. After downloading,
practising uses none at all.

**Will it work on my phone?**
It is built for ordinary Android phones, not expensive ones. Android first, because that
is what most people in the region actually use.

**Do I have to pay?**
Learning your own community's language is free, and will stay free. Paid features are
aimed at people abroad with the ability to pay, and their subscriptions help keep it free
for everyone else.

**I speak the language but I am not a teacher. Can I still help?**
Yes — that is exactly who this needs. You do not need qualifications to speak your own
language. Reading a phrase aloud is a real contribution.

**What if people disagree about the right way to say something?**
Often both are right in their own variety. Where they genuinely differ, we record both
and label where each is from, rather than declaring a winner.

**Which language will be first?**
Not yet decided. It will be chosen with a community that wants to partner on it — not
selected for them. If that could be your community, we would like to hear from you.

**Can this work for my language too?**
That is the intention. The software is open and free for any language community to use.
The pilot proves the approach; the design is meant to be repeated.
